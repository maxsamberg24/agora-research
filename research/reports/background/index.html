The anxious generation grew up

Every national statistic in the summary and presentation, what it measures, and where it comes from. Figures are listed in the order they appear in the presentation.

Loneliness and mental health

73–79%

of Gen Z report loneliness, the highest of any generation.

From Cigna’s national survey of more than 10,000 U.S. adults, fielded July–August 2019. Gen Z was defined as ages 18–22. 79% scored as lonely on the UCLA Loneliness Scale; 73% said they sometimes or always feel alone. Cigna’s 2025 follow-up put Gen Z loneliness at 67%, still the highest of any generation.

Appears inSummary · Presentation, slide 3

SourceCigna, Loneliness and the Workplace: 2020 U.S. Report (via CNBC); Cigna, Loneliness in America (2025)

61%

of 18–25-year-olds report serious loneliness.

Harvard’s Making Caring Common project surveyed U.S. adults in October 2020. “Serious loneliness” means feeling lonely frequently or almost all the time in the prior four weeks. Fieldwork was during the pandemic.

Appears inPresentation, slide 3

SourceHarvard Making Caring Common, Loneliness in America (2021)

29–36%

of 18–25-year-olds report depression (29%) and anxiety (36%).

About twice the rate reported by teenagers in the same survey (15% and 18%). National survey fielded December 2022.

Appears inPresentation, slide 3

SourceHarvard Making Caring Common, On Edge (2023)

58%

of 18–25-year-olds say they lack meaning or purpose in their lives.

Share reporting little or no meaning or purpose in the prior month. Same survey as above.

Appears inPresentation, slide 3

SourceHarvard Making Caring Common, On Edge (2023)

Time with other people

12.8 → 5.0

hours a week spent with friends, ages 18–29, 2010 to 2025.

Most of the decline came before COVID: the figure had already fallen to 6.5 hours by 2019. It dropped to 4.2 hours in 2020 and has recovered only to about 5.

Appears inSummary · Presentation, slide 4

SourceInstitute for Family Studies, Young adults are spending less time with friends (October 2025)

60 → 35 min

a day spent socializing in person, ages 15–24, 2005 to 2025.

The steepest decline of any age group over the period. From the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ American Time Use Survey.

Appears inPresentation, slide 4

SourceAxios, Americans of all ages are spending less time socializing (July 2026); BLS American Time Use Survey

33% → 13%

of Americans say they have ten or more close friends, 1990 to 2021.

Over the same period, the share with no close friends rose from 3% to 12%.

Appears inPresentation, slide 4

SourceSurvey Center on American Life, The State of American Friendship (2021)

Health

50%

higher likelihood of survival for people with stronger social relationships.

A meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 people, followed for an average of 7.5 years. The study reports this as a 50% greater likelihood of survival for people with stronger relationships. The summary and slide 5 state the same finding from the other side: people with weak relationships are more likely to die early.

Appears inSummary · Presentation, slide 5

SourceHolt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton, Social Relationships and Mortality Risk, PLOS Medicine (2010)

Drinking and venues

54%

of U.S. adults say they drink alcohol, the lowest in Gallup’s trend.

Gallup began asking in 1939. 2026 was the second straight year at this record low. Fieldwork July 1–19, 2026.

Appears inPresentation, slide 6

SourceGallup, Americans’ Drinking Remains at Record Low (August 2026)

72% → 50%

of 18–34-year-olds drink alcohol, 2001 to 2026.

72% is Gallup’s 2001–2003 average. The figure was 58% in 2023 and 50% in 2026. Young adults went from the age group most likely to drink to the least likely.

Appears inSummary · Presentation, slide 6

SourceGallup (2023); Gallup (2026)

−20% / −33%

Establishment counts from the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages.

SourceFortune (August 2026), citing BLS QCEW
